## Key points

**Alex's principles (spoken; the first was cut off at the recording start)**
- **Two versions by client readiness.** Clients in "observer mode" that hold back data and systems but have ambition and funding get a light training version with more constraints and fewer commitments; the transformation version is for clients able to be proactive.
- **Extended discovery as a stated constraint.** It may take longer than on other Jumpstarts: real consultancy discovery of workflows and tools, likely many tools spread across individuals, to find real pains. Generic "here are the ways to enable" content without that is a risky program.
- **Deeper tailoring than before.** Clients pick modules from a menu (papers, citation and evidence; modeling; others), and each item is configured to the client's pain in that block, at least in the first engagements.
- **Three phases per module.** Week 1, build and configure after discovery (MCP integrations, environment, creating and testing skills). Week 2, a session showing that part of the workflow, then a week of using it on real work. Week 3, they present results while SoftServe shows the next phase.
- **Assignments are demos on real tasks they choose, not hackathons.** He is reluctant about one-day hackathons on synthetic tasks; an "acceleration award" can motivate if the client likes gamification.
- **Transformation version adds at least a month** of lighter guidance after the trainings: observe how work changes, surface hidden issues, then advise on process, role-model and org change. Sold as one holistic offer, not a separate second phase, because nobody can promise process transformation right after the sessions.

**Arek's view (SDLC Jumpstart field experience)**
- Agrees processes should not be redefined: people, researchers even more than engineers, accept only small adjustments, and only once shown they are worth it.
- **Length:** wary of programs longer than six weeks, "one team, one sale". Clients want results fast; extended discovery is hard to sell unless a SoftServe team already works for that client and the program rides on it.
- **Tailoring:** disagrees with fully custom. Given the duration and how projects get staffed ("we won't get super smart people from every project"), lead with repeatable quick wins that need no deep SME: processing previous experiment results, a skill that fetches several data sources, generating a report people actually want, a pipeline for running many experiment ideas quickly, a personal knowledge vault (Obsidian markdown notes as a second brain). An "Oxford-level" AI-heavy team won't be impressed; a team focused on its own work will.
- **Evidence:** the Jumpstarts that worked delivered general knowledge a little customized (DAX, transcript "DAXCO": how to set up a Cursor marketplace and write proper skills, on a process the client was already building). Those that dove into client-specific work hit a rabbit hole (transcript "paywalls", Payworks inferred): the client expected side-by-side implementation, not training, and SoftServe lacked the subject knowledge.
- **His model for an unfamiliar domain:** SoftServe brings the core AI capabilities; the client's SME explains what they want; together they frame it as a prompt or skill and iterate, e.g. on an auto-research pipeline.
- **His slides (a "speedrun"):** research steps common across organizations: framing, exploration and review (context engineering matters more here than in SDLC), experiment design (a skill proposing experiments, a harness, the execution pipeline), execution, validation and analysis, communication (reports people actually read and that feed later experiments). Timeline: access and a pre-discovery of data sources before the start, so agents are connected on day 1; week 1 aligns and validates assumptions with little build; weeks 2–5 still open (Adel's work parallelization, a technical presentation by Serhiy and Max). Order: access to knowledge first (build an MCP or connector if missing), then show what can be done with the data, then apply it to existing workflows and find bottlenecks with mentoring sessions; target small workflows; heavy data synthesis becomes an additional service; SoftServe's involvement falls in weeks 4–5 as the skill marketplace is handed over. Metrics: a ground-truth question set timed at start and end, groundedness in the client's data sources rather than model knowledge, rediscovery (reusing earlier results for a new direction), adoption of the skills, and trust (how much LLM output gets thrown away), measured at regular checkups.
- **Parallel, not sequential:** on the SDLC Jumpstart, running modules in sequence did not work; they had to run partly in parallel. He liked Oleh's idea of parallel categories.

**Oleh's concepts**
1. **A living research map**, like the Living SDLC map: list the client's current process step by step and show at the end which steps AI accelerated. (After Alex's feedback his slide moved to buckets.)
2. **Buckets of sessions to choose from:** foundation (AI basics: skills, AGENTS.md; may be skipped for advanced teams) · tools (extend them with skills, connectors, MCP servers) · automation (walk the process step by step, e.g. protein design: literature → previous experiments → decide what to simulate → simulate → test the fit → hand to the wet lab; check each step's tools; build pipelines together) · processes (planning, tracking, documentation, meeting notes, an Obsidian second brain).
3. **Measurement around tracer tasks:** tasks that recur from one research project to the next, measured for speed and correctness; plus adoption and how many AI outputs experts accept.
4. **A kit that improves program to program:** every asset built (skill, sub-agent, pipeline, MCP integration, automation, a domain UI such as a 3D structure viewer) goes into a library shown in presales; after learning the client's pipeline and tools, the program is composed from those building blocks.
