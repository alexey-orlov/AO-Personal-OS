# Hiring & recruiting craft

_status: live theme — sourcing, screening, deciding, and closing talent in the AI era_
_slug: hiring-and-recruiting-craft_
_updated: 2026-08-26 · 14 insights from 9 episodes_

## The throughline
Across five founders/talent leaders, the craft of hiring splits into distinct, addressable stages: sourcing (Snowflake/Lemkin promote existing internal 'tool nerds' into new roles rather than posting externally; Ward scopes a targeted top-1% list and relentlessly courts it rather than trusting a passive funnel), screening (Hodak distributes top-of-funnel judgment across 7-8 employee voters and uses AI-resistant homework scored on objective metrics; Ward leans on long work-sample trials as the strongest predictor of on-the-job success), deciding (Datadog's Pomel and Encord both argue for hiring and firing fast — act on early signal rather than rationalizing sunk cost), and closing (Ward treats closing as a continuous, team-orchestrated process — daily standups, personalized rituals — not a single offer-stage sales pitch). Two structural claims cut across all of this: recruiting itself deserves strategic, well-compensated ownership rather than being treated as scheduling overhead (Ward), and simple cultural filters — like PhotoRoom's English-fluency requirement — can proxy for harder-to-screen traits like global ambition. Hodak's continuous peer-weighted review (IGEN) extends the same distribute-and-quantify-judgment logic from hiring into ongoing performance management.

## Insights

### AI is redefining roles: GTM engineers and adaptable mindsets win
Rather than hiring more traditional business analysts, Snowflake is building GTM engineering roles and converting people from marketing ops and analytics into those positions; soft skills like curiosity, adaptability, and change management now trump narrow technical certifications. Hiring exercises ask candidates to solve real problems with AI tools to surface practical problem‑solving and learning ability. The implication is that career paths and recruiting tests must shift to evaluate adaptability and outcome orientation.

### Hire your GTM engineer from internal 'tool nerd' talent, not job boards
Lemkin recommends promoting an existing curious marketer who already hacks tools (Zapier, dashboards, integrations) into the GTM engineer role rather than posting externally and getting poor candidates. He notes these people already understand internal workflows, have built useful automations, and adapt quickly to new agentic tools—skills that didn't exist a year ago. Only look externally if no one inside the marketing team can play that role, because internal knowledge reduces ramp time and vendor-lock friction.

### Use language and rituals as filters to hire truly global, ambitious talent
PhotoRoom deliberately uses English in its Paris office and treats global reach as a hiring filter: if a candidate isn't comfortable working in English, they're unlikely to be committed to building a global company. The founders say PhotoRoom serves users in 180 countries, so linguistic and mindset filters help ensure new hires want the same scale. This tactic matters because early hires determine product ambition and execution; simple operational rules can screen for alignment on global aspirations.

### Hire fast and fire fast accelerates learning and prevents paralysis
He argues founders should hire good-looking candidates immediately and be prepared to fire quickly if they don't work out, rather than waiting for a 'perfect' hire. The mindset shortens feedback loops, increases iteration velocity, and avoids long-term damage from keeping poor fits; when a founder delays firing, the broader team often asks later, 'What took you so long?'
— Y Combinator · 2026-07-24 · guest: Olivier Pomel (Datadog) · [▶ 29:08](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vb1Gcn10enw&t=1748) · `pi-vb1Gcn10enw-03`

### Founders should cut bad hires quickly—trust your gut to avoid long sunk costs
They describe multiple rounds of hiring sales teams (first two failed, third worked) and recount a specific hire they kept for about nine months despite YC advice and a clear mismatch. The lesson: avoid rationalizing sunk costs; act on early signals and 'follow your gut' to make faster personnel changes, which preserves runway and team effectiveness.

### Distributed, company‑wide voting prevents recruiting bottlenecks
Instead of centralizing top‑of‑funnel screening, Hodak's team routes initial applications to a variable set of 7–8 employees to vote, which spreads load, averages judgment, and avoids single-person bottlenecks. This feeds into a four-step hiring funnel (company voting → phone screen → AI‑resistant homework → full interview) that yields measurable conversions (about 17% of top‑funnel apps go to phone screen) and preserves founder time while surfacing diverse perspectives. He prefers homeworks that are scorable by a few objective metrics so candidate results can be compared despite AI assistance.

### Continuous peer‑weighted reviews (IGEN) surface issues far sooner
To replace slow annual 360 reviews, Hodak's company asks colleagues every 4–6 weeks a single question — essentially 'would you hire this person again?' — and builds a graph where votes are weighted by voters' reputations (akin to PageRank/eigenvector centrality). They add Monte‑Carlo dropout to detect anomalous voting blocs; the resulting monthly signal highlights underperformers and cultural problems early, reducing the trauma and lag associated with yearly HR cycles and enabling faster corrective action.

### Treat every hire like an executive search for the top 1%
Instead of casting a wide funnel and hiring whoever drops out the bottom, Ward recommends scoping what "top 1%" means for your company, building a targeted list (he suggests thinking in terms of the top ~50 global fits), and relentlessly activating that list. That scoping sets sourcing, the interview agenda, and the pitch; market-mapping and probing trusted advisors then triangulate the same standout names. The payoff is predictable: you start by courting the highest-impact people rather than hoping one slips through a noisy funnel, which materially raises long-term talent density.

### Traditional recruiting funnels produce suboptimal 'remainder' hires
Ward calls the common model the "funnel of doom": reach out to a hundred people, only a fraction reply, and you end up hiring from that remainder rather than the true top candidates. He illustrates this with a simple 100→20 example to show why passive reply rates don't represent the best available talent and why we regress to the mean if we optimize that pipeline. Recognizing this explains why many companies plateau on talent quality and why a targeted, pillar-of-excellence approach is a superior alternative.
— Lenny's Podcast · 2026-08-09 · guest: Adam Ward (Head of Talent, Cursor) · [▶ 13:15](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zegYJ6dhIg4&t=795) · `pi-zegYJ6dhIg4-02`

### Work samples give the strongest signal of future success
Ward cites research and Cursor's practice that work samples or side-by-side project trials are the highest predictors of on-the-job success, so the company runs long, curated on-site projects and side-by-side technical sessions. These exercises are two-way: they provide hiring teams high-fidelity signal about skills and collaboration, and they give candidates realistic preview and self-selection power (Michael Terrell's experiment that removed trials tanked signal). For roles where fit matters beyond résumé badges, investing in work trials markedly reduces costly hiring mistakes.

### Closing is built throughout the process and requires a team
Rather than treating the offer as the moment to sell, Cursor 'closes' candidates continuously by discovering motivations early, orchestrating the right interviewers, and removing objections along the way; for high-profile targets they hold daily standups to coordinate outreach. They also use personal rituals — curated meals, small bespoke gifts (an example: a specialty instrument for a candidate who was a violinist), dedicated Slack channels, and pre-boarding touches like sending a laptop — to convert acceptances into joins. In a hyper-competitive market with reneges and counteroffers, sustained, personalized engagement is the difference between an offer and an actual hire.
— Lenny's Podcast · 2026-08-09 · guest: Adam Ward (Head of Talent, Cursor) · [▶ 62:17](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zegYJ6dhIg4&t=3737) · `pi-zegYJ6dhIg4-04`

### Recruiters must be empowered, strategic, and highly compensated
Ward argues recruiting should be treated as a strategic function: recruiters are the 'confidence engine' who quarterback the experience, curate interviewers, and keep hiring managers accountable, not mere schedulers. Because truly strategic recruiters are rare and have outsized impact on which people a company can attract, he believes organizations should compensate and empower them accordingly and avoid offloading hiring accountability to recruiting teams alone. Making recruiting a first-class, well-resourced capability is essential when talent is the leading lever of company success.

### Hire by watching candidates manage agents — require a recorded build
Instead of early-stage interviews, Carson asks applicants to submit a full-screen recording of themselves building a feature — letting him evaluate how they use agents and manage workflows without an initial meeting. He then advances promising hires to a paid task inside Devon so he can watch real agent-managed work and review the replay. The method prioritizes practical agent-management skill over resumes or in-person chemistry and mirrors how the company will actually operate.

### Hire for upward trajectory and cultural fit over impressive resumes
Legora abandoned chasing fancy logos on CVs and instead prioritized candidates with high growth potential and cultural alignment; their top seller is a 23‑year‑old with no prior sales background who has sold over $10M. They codified three core values (Lean in, Fight for excellence, Grow together—LFG), maintained hands‑on interview practices during scale, and use Stockholm onboarding to transmit culture globally. The consequence: a self-selecting, high‑velocity team able to out-execute larger incumbents.
