# Resource Abundance: Nuclear Microreactors, Minerals Mining & Ocean Robotics

_status: live theme — company case studies on extracting/producing scarce physical resources (energy, minerals, ocean materials) via vertically-integrated, autonomy-first hardware ventures_
_slug: resource-abundance-nuclear-minerals-ocean_
_updated: 2026-08-25 · 18 insights from 5 episodes_

## The throughline
Three a16z "American Dynamism" companies and a second Kalanick interview converge on the same operating model: scarce physical resources — energy, minerals, ocean access — get unlocked not by a single breakthrough technology but by vertically-integrated, software-and-autonomy-first hardware companies that treat the whole extraction/production chain as one system. Radiant factory-builds transportable ~1MW microreactors to sidestep the site-by-site unpredictability that stalled US nuclear iteration after the 1970s; Mariana Minerals pairs autonomous haul trucks with "Mariana OS" to replace ~150 site spreadsheets and race a 15-year copper-demand cliff while China holds over 90% of critical-mineral refining; Ulysses is supply- rather than demand-limited for its swap-in-20-minutes modular underwater vehicles, driven by both ecosystem-restoration cost pressure and a shift toward cheap, distributed maritime threats. Kalanick's own mining thesis — mining as an "industrial computer," wheeled rather than humanoid robots as the automation form factor — applies the same vertical-integration-plus-autonomy logic a second time, independent of the three American Dynamism companies.

## Insights

### Mass-produced nuclear microreactors are positioned as a civilization enabler
Moriana Minerals (via Turner Caldwell) pitches mass-produced microreactors that are "reliable in the most wild scenario" as not just an energy solution but a strategic enabler for human expansion beyond Earth. The argument links production-scale, rugged reactors to both terrestrial resilience and the long-term ability to leave our star, framing such hardware as necessary infrastructure for civilization-scale goals. The claim is ambitious: scalable, dependable reactors could unlock missions and settlements that current power systems cannot support.

### The US stopped iterating on nuclear because it stopped listening to customers
Radiant argues the U.S. ceased meaningful nuclear innovation after the 1960s–70s — the last reactor at Idaho National Lab was built in 1977 — because public perception, advocacy, and ever-tightening regulation made construction unpredictable and expensive. The company claims this regulatory and market disconnect turned nuclear into a niche, and that regaining iterative, customer-driven development (i.e., smaller, repeatable products) is necessary to change the future of energy and make nuclear affordable and deployable again.
— a16z · 2026-08-05 · guest: — · [▶ 1:03](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-9Vh7tsgM7Y&t=63) · `pi--9Vh7tsgM7Y-01`

### A transportable ~1 MW microreactor can replace diesel generators for years
Radiant's product is a transportable microreactor that fits on an 18-wheeler, delivers roughly five years of continuous power without fuel resupply, and outputs many times the power of a diesel generator; units can be daisy-chained (e.g., four units could power a small city or a very large data center). That makes it useful wherever you would use portable diesel — remote bases, disaster zones, off-grid data centers or rapid military/AI deployments — while eliminating on-site refueling and many logistical burdens.
— a16z · 2026-08-05 · guest: — · [▶ 5:34](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-9Vh7tsgM7Y&t=334) · `pi--9Vh7tsgM7Y-02`

### Factory-built reactors remove site uncertainty and lower customer friction
Radiant insists on building reactors in a controlled factory to avoid the cost and timeline unpredictability of digging sites and on-site construction — once a reactor is site-installed it becomes a radiological site forever, complicating customers' willingness to host it. By shifting complexity into repeatable factory processes (analogous to how Apple removed friction with the iPhone), customers see near-zero on-site uncertainty and predictable costs, enabling nuclear to become a commodity rather than a bespoke civil engineering project.
— a16z · 2026-08-05 · guest: — · [▶ 4:49](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-9Vh7tsgM7Y&t=289) · `pi--9Vh7tsgM7Y-03`

### Radiant aims to be first to run a new commercial microreactor in decades
No company has yet turned on a new commercial microreactor; Radiant was selected to test its prototype in the historic Idaho National Laboratory dome and plans to go critical in a full-power test by 2026, with design targets above 700°C and 150 continuous test hours. Demonstrating a rapid, safe deployment at a national lab is positioned as proof that microreactors can be built and operated quickly, unlocking policy support and customer confidence needed for wider adoption.

### The minerals industry hasn't adopted modern tech holistically
The speakers argue the minerals sector is ripe for disruption because no one has yet applied today's modern technologies across the whole value chain. They present this as an underexploited opportunity: combining robotics, sensing, and systems engineering could materially change how minerals are found, extracted, and managed. Recognizing the sector-level technology gap reframes mining as a software-and-systems problem, not just geology and heavy equipment.

### We must mine more copper in 15 years than before
Caldwell claims the upcoming demand for copper is unprecedented — "we need to mine more copper in the next 15 years than we have in all of human history since the Bronze Age." This is driven by electrification, EVs, data centers, batteries and other infrastructure; meeting that pace requires far faster project delivery and higher throughput than the legacy industry provides. If supply doesn't scale, many physical technologies simply can't be built because the raw metal units won't exist.
— a16z · 2026-08-05 · guest: Turner Caldwell (Mariana Minerals) · [▶ 0:48](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uFt-tkagUUI&t=48) · `pi-uFt-tkagUUI-01`

### Over 90% of critical-mineral refining is concentrated in China
The transcript states that more than 90% of the critical minerals used today are refined in China, a transition that happened rapidly over recent decades. That geographic concentration is framed as a direct national-security and supply-chain risk: if a single country controls refining, Western economies lack self-determination to manufacture key hardware. The implication is policy and capital must back domestic/friendly refining capacity, not just raw mining.
— a16z · 2026-08-05 · guest: Turner Caldwell (Mariana Minerals) · [▶ 1:05](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uFt-tkagUUI&t=65) · `pi-uFt-tkagUUI-02`

### Vertical integration plus software is the practical way to rebuild supply
Mariana's core claim is that the only scalable response is a vertically integrated, software-first mining and refining company that combines atoms and bits. They argue software — Mariana OS — removes manual paper-and-spreadsheet workflows, gives small teams leverage, and coordinates dynamic operations; evidence includes finding roughly 150 disparate spreadsheets on a single site and deploying automation to cut those inefficiencies. Vertical integration lets them capture real-world feedback loops (mining ↔ refining ↔ software) to accelerate improvements that contractors or siloed actors won't achieve.

### A robust autonomy stack is required for remote and off-world mining
Caldwell points out that mining the ocean floor or extraterrestrial bodies (Mars, moon, asteroids) will be infeasible without a mature autonomy stack, and that the same autonomy is valuable on Earth. Mariana is deliberately building that autonomy because it accelerates deployment, enables rapid iteration (they deliberately started with two autonomous haul trucks to surface failure modes quickly), and is a strategic capability that scales beyond a single mine. In short, autonomy is both a productivity multiplier now and a prerequisite for future high-impact mining frontiers.

### Ulysses was founded to build underwater robots for one specific problem
Ulysses began three years ago with a narrow, operational focus: build underwater robots that solve a particular, practical challenge. The emphasis on a concrete, solvable problem guides product development rather than aiming immediately for a broad platform — a classic startup approach to de-risk engineering before scaling. That origin explains why their roadmap emphasizes reliable fielded systems rather than speculative capabilities.

### Ulysses' real ambition is a 24/7 network of sea vehicles
Beyond a single robot, the company's broader vision is a persistent network of surface and undersea vehicles working around the clock to perform critical ocean tasks. They explicitly frame the goal as continuous, distributed operations — vehicles that coordinate to "solve these critical tasks all around the ocean" — which implies investments in autonomy, communications, and fleet management rather than one-off hardware. If achieved, that transforms many marine industries from episodic to continuous monitoring and intervention.

### Demand already outstrips production for ocean robots
Ulysses reports it is "supply-limited instead of demand-limited," meaning customers across defense, offshore industry, and ecosystem restoration want reliable, affordable underwater vehicles faster than Ulysses can produce them. That reflects broad interest driven by needs like cable installation, critical-mineral access, and mine clearing — not a niche market — so scaling manufacturing is a binding constraint, not market demand.

### Standardized modular hardware enables fast field reconfiguration
Ulysses designed vehicles with standardized power and data interfaces so customers can swap sensors, batteries, or thrusters in the field; a multi-beam sonar can be attached "within 20 minutes." This modularity lowers cost per mission, lets a single base vehicle serve defense, industry, and restoration uses, and accelerates iteration because new capabilities can be tested and deployed without bespoke designs.

### Maritime conflict is shifting to distributed, low-cost asymmetric attacks
The video argues warfare at sea is becoming "increasingly asymmetric," where small, inexpensive systems (mines, unmanned craft, cable-tapping tools) can threaten high-value assets like tankers and undersea infrastructure. Examples cited include mines in the Strait of Hormuz and attacks on pipelines and cables, implying traditional expensive naval systems are insufficient and militaries will demand large numbers of unmanned, cheap platforms.

### Seagrass restoration spurred an in-house, low-cost robotics approach
The company began by trying to mechanize seagrass planting after seeing crews toss seeds from boats; off-the-shelf unmanned underwater vehicles were "multiple orders of magnitude higher" in price and unaffordable. That origin explains Ulysses' focus on cost reduction, in-house machining for rapid prototyping, and field-driven product design to meet practical restoration needs.

### Automating mining boosts output, cuts opex, and improves safety
Adams' mining thesis is that industrial automation can raise yield (he asks mining CEOs if they'd like '20% more gold per year'), reduce operating expenses, and make dangerous sites safer by replacing manual processes with robotics and software orchestration. He presents mining as a full 'industrial computer' where land is storage, trucks are the network, and processing/flotation plants are the CPU—citing a lithium plant and massive autonomous haul vehicles as examples. This matters because minerals and energy are foundational to semiconductors and AI, so automating extraction is both strategically critical and a large commercial opportunity.

### Industrial autonomy will use specialized wheeled robots, not humanoids
Kalanick argues high‑scale industrial tasks favor specialized machines with wheels ('wheelbase for robots') rather than general humanoid robots; mobility is the dominant requirement and wheels simplify autonomy and payload handling. He positions autonomy as a platform that must be developed in‑house rather than outsourced—because many industrial markets (food delivery, parcel, trucking, off‑road/mining) are distinct 'silver‑medal' opportunities beyond ride‑sharing. The implication: build domain‑specific autonomous platforms fast to capture dozens of large vertical markets.
