## The throughline
Sonnet 5.5's launch-week verdict from Every's live session: the low and medium effort settings are the sweet spot — fast, cheap, and good enough for iterative design and even single-shot coding tasks once thought Opus-only — while high effort slows and turns inconsistent, and Opus still leads on long-form writing and top-quality tool calls. (synthesis)

## Insights

### Sonnet 5.5 Low/Medium are excellent for iterative design work
The hosts repeatedly found Sonnet 5.5's low and medium effort settings to be the 'sweet spot' for product design and engineering tasks: they are fast, cheap, and produce surprisingly high-quality outputs for UI fixes, Figma-to-code conversions, and repeated design tweaks. Multiple team members reported switching much of their day-to-day work to Sonnet Low/Medium because it lets them iterate quickly without paying Opus-level costs or waiting for slower, higher-effort runs. This matters because designers and engineers benefit more from many quick iterations than from fewer very high-quality outputs.

### Sonnet 5.5 widens access to experimentation by lowering runtime cost
Because Sonnet Low/Medium uses fewer tokens and runs faster, the hosts argue it enables people with limited subscription budgets to experiment more — running many more trials than they could with Opus or higher-tier models. They point out that this will let users do things they previously reserved for expensive plans (e.g., running many design or code iterations) and thus broaden who can adopt AI-driven workflows. The practical consequence: cheaper iterations make AI an everyday tool rather than only a high-cost, occasional assistant.

### At high effort settings Sonnet becomes slow and less usable than Opus
When pushed to high or very-high effort, Sonnet 5.5 slows dramatically and produces inconsistent results (hosts describe features disappearing or mechanics changing unexpectedly in a game project), which makes Opus a better pick for big one-shot feature builds. They illustrated this with a game and a long audio-imparted run where Sonnet removed elements (like fans or a cat) after earlier successes, and with benchmarks showing run-times and repetition count exploding at higher settings. The practical takeaway: use Sonnet for iterative, repeated work and Opus for heavy, tool-dependent, or single-run feature completions.

### Sonnet 5.5 can perform single-shot coding and creative tasks previously unique to Opus
In some tests the hosts ran single-shot benchmarks (LMG Bench) and end-to-end code tasks where Sonnet Low completed code generation, verification, and running in one shot — something previously notable about Opus. They demonstrated creative programming outputs (a Rubik's-cube-like logo, game pieces, and a small golf game) produced from short prompts, showing Sonnet's practical capability to execute substantial creative/code work at lower effort levels. That means teams can sometimes replace expensive runs with Sonnet for full pipeline tasks, not just toy examples.

### For long-form writing and highest-quality tool-calls, Opus (or other models) still lead
Tests by Katie and Mike showed Opus 5.5 and other writing-focused models remain slightly smarter and better for substantive writing and complex tool-calling; Sonnet is a competent fallback when you hit quota limits. The hosts recommend using Opus or Claude/Codex for computer-heavy workflows and deep writing work, and using Sonnet when you need a cheaper, fast assistant for more routine drafting or when your primary model's usage limits are reached. So Sonnet is additive — it increases capacity rather than fully replacing higher-tier models.
