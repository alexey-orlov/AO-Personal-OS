## Executive summary

## Top items to study this session

#### 1. 🟡 Missing / wrong article on singular count nouns — 15 instances

Every singular countable noun in English needs a determiner — *a*, *the*, *my*, *this*. Dropping it is the most frequent and most audible pattern in this call, and it appears in exactly the places where you're building credibility (describing Creatio, describing org structure).

- "they started as **the** CRM vendor" → they started as **a** CRM vendor
- "There is **CTO and CPO**" → There's **a CTO and a CPO**
- "so initially Creatio just had tooling for sales and service automation with **heavy** business process automation engine" → with **a heavy** business process automation engine

**Drill:** Before any singular noun, run a two-second check: *a* (one of many), *the* (the specific one we both know), or a possessive. If none fits, the noun is probably wrong — you likely need the plural. Rehearse on your own bio: *a CRM vendor · the marketing piece · a no-code platform · an overview · a kind of matrix*.

#### 2. 🔴 Run-on / over-nested answers — 4 critical, 6 moderate

Your strongest content is buried in your longest sentences. Twice the listener genuinely loses the thread: the third-party-developer answer runs ~90 words through five clauses, and the enterprise-vs-SMB answer stacks a caveat inside a caveat. This costs more than any grammar error, because a recruiter relaying your answers to the CEO can only relay what she followed.

- "the crucial component in the puzzle that was missing … to enable that transition was to have third-party applications, third-party application developers coming in and deciding to build solutions on top of Creatio's platform so that at the end the entire offering … covers lots of different horizontals … you name the workflow, you name the industry" → **"The missing piece was third-party developers. Once outside teams built on our platform, the offering covered horizontals and verticals far beyond CRM — HR automation, warehouse automation, anything."**
- "It's super important if we're talking about the expansion initiative, something that's new. To the portfolio, this kind of breaks existing processes, not that fit in existing processes, then you'd rather have that separated" → **"For a genuinely new expansion initiative — one that breaks existing processes rather than fitting them — separate it out with maximum autonomy."**

**Drill:** One idea, then a full stop. When you feel a "so that…", "because…", or "which…" coming as the third connector in a sentence, stop and start a new one. Target: no spoken sentence over ~25 words.

#### 3. 🔴 Non-native verb + complement patterns — 7 instances

English verbs are picky about what follows them. *Imply* never takes object + infinitive; *emphasize* doesn't pair with *highly*; *report* goes *to*, not *into*; *elaborate* needs *on*.

- "It **implies you to be** really lean and innovative" → It **requires you to be** really lean and innovative
- "you **highly emphasize** those jacks of all trades" → you **put a lot of emphasis on** those jacks of all trades
- "a different division **reporting into** me" → a different division **reporting to** me

**Drill:** Memorize the four fixes as fixed strings: *require someone to do* · *put emphasis on* · *report to* · *elaborate on*. Also: *think like an entrepreneur* (not *as*), *look at going upmarket* (not *look into*).

#### 4. 🟡 Past narration slipping into present — 7 instances

When you narrate a finished chapter (Creatio, Jooble), keep the whole passage in the simple past. You also reach for the present perfect for closed periods, which English reserves for open-ended ones.

- "It **includes** marketing, sales, and service" → It **included** marketing, sales, and service
- "the products **I've been dealing with**" → the products **I dealt with**
- "exactly the journey **I've had** at Creatio" → exactly the journey **I had** at Creatio

**Drill:** Job you left = simple past, no exceptions. Present perfect only for "in my career so far" statements: *I've built two product orgs* ✓ / *I've had that journey at Creatio* ✗ → *I had*.

#### 5. 🔴 Mangled or misused set phrases — 5 instances

Fixed English phrases don't survive rearranging. "The more is less approach" inverts a well-known idiom and makes the listener stop; "in the first place" means *to begin with / why at all*, not *firstly*.

- "using the **more is less** approach" → using the **less is more** approach
- "my role was to, **in the first place**, set up the platform layer" → my role was **first** to set up the platform layer
- "so that **at the end** the entire offering… covers" → so that **in the end** the entire offering covers

**Drill:** Lock these four: *less is more · in the end · at first glance · on top of that* (not *on the other side of that*).

#### 6. 🔴 Russian calques — 3 instances

*Feel myself* is a direct carry-over of *чувствую себя* and is the one item here that a native listener will visibly register — in English it also carries an unintended connotation. *As for* is grammatical, but you use it where a plain preposition is needed.

- "a sweet spot that **I feel myself confident in**" → a sweet spot **I feel confident in**
- "things that I am really focusing on when looking for something **as for** my next step" → …when looking **for** my next step
- "**as for me**, that's super crucial" → **for me**, that's crucial

**Drill:** *Feel* + adjective takes no object: *I feel confident · I feel good · I feel at home*. Never *I feel myself* + anything.

#### 7. 🟡 "how it looks like" → "what it looks like" — 3 instances

*Like* needs an object, and *what* supplies it; *how* already carries the manner, so the two can't combine. This one recurred every time you described process work — high-frequency in exactly your vocabulary.

- "**how** this process **looks like** in your product" → **what** this process **looks like** in your product
- "raise the bar in terms of **how** the artifacts should **look like**" → in terms of **what** the artifacts should **look like**

**Drill:** Two valid forms, never mixed: *How does it look?* / *What does it look like?* Say both aloud three times, then drop *how…like* permanently.

#### 8. 🟡 Direct questions need subject–verb inversion — 2 instances

In a real question the verb comes before the subject — including in the second half of an *or* question, where the inversion is easy to forget.

- "So what type of customers **those are**?" → So what type of customers **are those**?
- "or **it's** also part of customer success?" → or **is it** also part of customer success?

**Drill:** After "or", re-invert: *Do they have support as a separate team, or **is it** part of customer success?*
