## Top items to study this session

#### 1. 🟡 Missing article before a noun — 9 instances

Every singular countable noun and every specific, already-mentioned noun needs *a* or *the*. This is the single most frequent marker of a Slavic-language speaker in English, and it fires most often on product/platform names and inside prepositional phrases.

- "our solutions we built on **AIDP** platform" → "on **the AIDP** platform"
- "it can be licensing outside **marketplace**" → "outside **the marketplace**"
- "It might be Terraform, might be **virtual machine**, it might be just **Kubernetes container**" → "might be **a virtual machine**, it might be just **a Kubernetes container**"

**Drill:** before any noun, ask two questions in order — "one of many?" → *a*; "the one we already talked about?" → *the*. Platform and product names in a phrase almost always take *the*: *the Marketplace*, *the AIDP platform*, *the US*.

#### 2. 🟡 Missing preposition after a verb or adjective — 6 instances

English verbs and adjectives carry a fixed preposition that RU/UA either drops (because the case ending does the job) or assigns differently. Getting it wrong is immediately audible.

- "Maybe you can **point me on** those contacts" → "Maybe you can **point me to** those contacts"
- "it's a hassle to find the right **people to talk**" → "the right **people to talk to**"
- "I'm a little bit **surprised in** the setup" → "**surprised by** the setup"

**Drill:** memorise the pair, not the verb — *point to*, *rely on*, *surprised by*, *issues with*, *talk to*, *as I understand **it***. When a relative clause ends in a verb ("people to talk"), check whether the preposition got stranded off the end.

#### 3. 🔴 Run-on / over-nested sentence — 3 instances

Three of your questions ran 50–60 words with four or five clauses stacked before the point. The listener has to hold everything in memory and often loses the actual question — twice Speaker B answered only the last clause.

- "And actually, we don't have an answer what's the best option for us yet because we are going to plan that strategy, but we need to understand how the channel works because if you say nobody purchase stuff from the marketplace directly…" → "**We haven't decided which option fits us yet. First we need to know how the channel works: does anyone actually buy from the marketplace directly, or is it just visibility?**"
- "As far as I understand from the documentation, in order to submit and get the application published, we need to have the resources, for example, certain types of GPUs that are needed for certain types of workloads provisioned in our tenancy, even though we are not using it." → "**The documentation says we have to provision the GPUs our workload needs in our own tenancy before we can publish — even though we never run them. Is that right?**"

**Drill:** two sentences, then stop. If a sentence has passed three "and / because / but / where", you've gone too far — split it and put the question first: *"One question, then the background."*

#### 4. 🟡 Yes/no question without inversion — 4 instances

In English a yes/no question inverts the subject and the verb, even when it's the second half of an *or*. Keeping statement word order turns it into a statement with a question mark, and listeners often don't realise you asked anything.

- "Because sometimes they do that… **or it's not the case** with Oracle here?" → "**or is that not the case** with Oracle?"
- "will we be able to manage that early next week, **or it's going to be** a long journey?" → "**or is it going to be** a long haul?"
- "So are we free to pick whatever we want, or like, **there are some pitfalls**?" → "**or are there pitfalls**?"

**Drill:** after every *"or…"* in a question, flip the next two words: *or is it*, *or are there*, *or was he*, *or do they*. Never *or it is*, *or there are*.

#### 5. 🟡 Non-native verb-noun collocation — 4 instances

The grammar is fine but the verb isn't the one English pairs with that noun. It reads as translated rather than wrong.

- "I can **bring out** some context of where we stand" → "I can **give** some context on where we stand"
- "it does not really **stick to** OCI" → "it isn't really **tied to** OCI"
- "questions that really **deserve** your expertise" → "questions that really **need** your expertise"

**Drill:** *give/provide context*, *tied to a platform*, *need someone's expertise*, *list something for free*. When you're translating a verb from Russian, check whether the English noun already has its own verb.

#### 6. 🔴 Word order collapses when the sentence gets complex — 2 instances

When a sentence carries a long object plus a modal plus a phrasal verb, the pieces come out in RU/UA order and the listener has to re-parse.

- "I think definitely **all technical questions we will need them sort it out**" → "I think we'll definitely **need to sort out all the technical questions**"
- "So **you're just interested in those leads because they are not there on the marketplace**" → "So **you're just not counting on leads from there**, because customers don't come through the marketplace"

**Drill:** English is strictly subject → verb → object. Say the verb before the long object, not after it: *"we need to sort out X"*, never *"X we need sort out"*. If the object is longer than five words, that's the warning sign.

#### 7. 🔴 "suggest me" — verb that can't take an indirect object — 1 instance

*Suggest*, *explain* and *recommend* never take a bare indirect object in English — and here the verb you wanted was *tell* or *correct*.

- "Federico, **suggest me if** I got you right" → "Federico, **tell me if** I've got this right" / "**correct me if** I'm wrong"

**Drill:** *tell me*, *show me*, *give me* — yes. *suggest me*, *explain me*, *recommend me* — never; use *suggest **to** me* or, better, a different verb.

#### 8. 🔴 *installment* ≠ *installation* — 1 instance

*Installment* means a payment in parts or an episode of a series. In a conversation about billing and credits, using it for "installing software" actively misleads.

- "there are some listings that are not leading to **installment**" → "listings that don't lead to an **installation**"

**Drill:** *install → installation* (putting software on a machine); *installment* only ever = *pay in installments*. Safest spoken form: "listings that don't get installed."
