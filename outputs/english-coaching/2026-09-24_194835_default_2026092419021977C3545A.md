### Top items to study this session

#### 1. 🟡 Run-on / overlong sentence — 3 instances

Your two main contributions each took 60–110 words, with the same idea repeated inside them. For a senior exec audience, asking first and adding context after lands better and sounds more senior.

- "And you might have some understanding of the customers and the signals and the demand but don't have the capacity for that and don't have the capacity to consider hundreds, tens of those… solutions, and you can just, maybe we can say, like, offload those things to us because you understand the demand but you don't have the capacity." → **You see the demand but don't have the capacity to build dozens of industry solutions — so you can offload them to us.**
- "So looking forward, I would extend that to the statement that probably the ideal scenario and ideal expectation from our end would be understanding the roadmap of those use cases and us focusing on building those packs actually, right?" → **Ideally, we'd know your use-case roadmap and focus on building those packs ourselves.**
- "So we really want to understand in this setup where we have the OCI infrastructure, NVIDIA VSS, AAQ, and QOP engines… Where do you see currently the added value of that template layer…? And if you have the vision… Could you please share that vision…" → **Where do you see the value of the template layer between NVIDIA and our solutions, and how do you plan to grow it?**

**Drill:** Before a long turn, decide on the one sentence you want them to remember and say it first. If a word comes up for the third time in one sentence ("capacity"), stop and end the sentence there.

#### 2. 🟡 Wrong preposition in collocation — 3 instances

Russian/Ukrainian carry-over: *вопрос к вам* → "question **to** you". In English, questions and feedback go **for** someone, and the set phrase is "at this point **in** time."

- "at this point **of** time" → at this point **in** time
- "it's not a question **to** you guys" → it's not a question **for** you guys
- "we might have some feedback **to** you" → we might have some feedback **for** you

**Drill:** *a question for you*, *feedback for you*, *a request for you*. But you *send / give feedback to* someone. Set phrase: *at this point in time*.

#### 3. 🟢 Articles (missing, extra, wrong) — 5 instances

Each slip is small, but five in a 30-minute call add up. One rule covers most of them: use *the* when both sides know which one you mean; use *a* for "one of many."

- "combining **the both** messages" → combining **both** messages
- "based on **Bosch** engagement" → based on **the Bosch** engagement
- "here is **a** status quo" → here is **the** status quo

**Drill:** *both* never takes *the* before it. A named project is "the Bosch engagement," "the Sky project." *Status quo* always takes *the*.

#### 4. 🟡 Indirect question — no inversion — 2 instances

A question embedded inside another sentence keeps statement word order: no question-style verb flip. For yes/no choices, add *whether*.

- "do you have that understanding of what **are the areas** where you might need partners" → do you have a sense of **which areas** you might need partners **in**
- "how much the… strategy is really leaning towards NVIDIA packs, **or you kind of consider** solutions where we are not using NVIDIA" → …or **whether you also consider** solutions without NVIDIA

**Drill:** "Can you tell me what the areas **are**?", not "what **are** the areas". "I wonder **whether** you consider…", not "or you consider…".

#### 5. 🟡 "would" in the if-clause — 1 instance

In hypothetical conditionals, *would* goes in the result clause, never in the *if* clause. This one rule prevents a whole family of slips.

- "if you**'d ask** me how much we rely on the packs" → if you **asked** me how much we rely on the packs

**Drill:** *If you asked me, I'd say…* / *If we had more data, we'd know.* The *if* half uses the past tense, and the other half uses *would*.
