### Top items to study this session

#### 1. 🟡 Direct question: auxiliary before the subject — 4 instances

In a direct question, *is / are / do / does* comes before the subject, and every alternative after *or* needs it again. Russian and Ukrainian can build a question with intonation alone, so the second half of your either/or questions slid back into statement order, and *maybe you have any questions?* is a statement with a question mark (a calque of *может, у вас есть вопросы?*).

- "**or there are** some … access and other questions to be figured out" → **or are there** some … access and other questions to be figured out
- "**or it just works** out of the box" → **or does it just work** out of the box
- "Guys, Karsten, Paweł, **maybe you have** any questions from your end?" → Guys, Karsten, Paweł, **do you have** any questions from your end?

**Drill:** *Do we just need X, or are there other steps, or does it just work?* · *Is it part of AIDP, or is it a standalone tool?* · *Do you have any questions?* / *Any questions from your end?* Check: after *or* in a question, the next word is *is / are / do / does*, not *it / there*.

#### 2. 🟡 Overlong sentence when asking a question — 3 instances

Say the question in one short sentence, then add the context as a separate one. Three of your sentences around a question ran 40–60 words each: three openers stacked before a summary, three options where the first and the third asked the same thing, and a premise with nested clauses before the actual question.

- "So for this integration with AIDP to work for us through MCP, **do we just need that AIDP provisioned in our tenancy**, or there are some, I don't know, access and other questions to be figured out somehow with our instance, **or it just works out of the box** as we are set with the tenancy?" → For the MCP integration with AIDP: **once AIDP is provisioned in our tenancy, does it just work out of the box**, or are there access or other questions to sort out first?
- "**And from my understanding, did I get it correctly, so** the whole thing that you do in this, in the process of preparing the demo is you create with the help of Codex the data … and you show them here." → **So if I understand correctly,** to prepare the demo you use Codex to create the data for the customer-specific use cases and the typical queries users would run, then you show them here.
- "Based on what you've shown, it looks like typically **you are in a similar situation that we also have** with many enterprise customers where you don't have any meaningful inputs about the use case they care at the very beginning." → From what you've shown, **you're often where we are** with many enterprise customers: at the start, there's no meaningful input on which use case they care about.

**Drill:** Question first, under 15 words; context after, as its own sentence. Before you say *or*, check that the next option is really different from the first. One opener per question.

#### 3. 🟡 Check-your-understanding opener — 2 instances

English checks understanding with a few fixed openers: *Just to confirm…*, *So if I understand correctly…*, *Did I get that right?* One of yours matched none of them (*just to be able to say*), and the other stacked three framings before the content (*from my understanding, did I get it correctly, so*).

- "And then **just to be able to say**, you do that all with Codex" → And then, **just to confirm**, you do that all with Codex
- "And **from my understanding, did I get it correctly, so** the whole thing that you do…" → **So if I understand correctly,** the whole thing that you do…

**Drill:** One opener per question: *Just to confirm, …?* · *Just so I'm clear, …?* · *So if I understand correctly, …* · or close with *Did I get that right?* When *get* means "understand", it's *get it right*, not *get it correctly*.

#### 4. 🟡 Preposition stays at the end of a relative clause — 1 instance

When a verb takes a preposition (*care about*, *work with*, *talk to*), the preposition stays at the end of a relative clause even though its object has moved to the front. Russian and Ukrainian put it before the relative word (*о котором*, *про який*), so in English it tends to fall off.

- "the use case they **care** at the very beginning" → the use case they **care about** at the very beginning

**Drill:** *the use case they care about* · *the team we work with* · *the client we talked to* · *the platform it runs on*. Check: say the clause as a full sentence (*they care about the use case*); the preposition you hear there stays at the end.

#### 5. 🟡 Verb and object stay together — 1 instance

English keeps the verb next to its object and puts the "how" (*with the help of Codex*) before the verb or after the object. A long phrase in between makes the listener wait for the object; Russian word order allows it, English does not.

- "you **create with the help of Codex the data** to support the customer-specific use cases" → you **use Codex to create the data** to support the customer-specific use cases

**Drill:** *use X to do Y*: *we use Codex to generate the dataset*. Or verb + object + how: *we built the demo with Codex*. Never verb + how + object.

#### 6. 🟡 *It makes sense for us to…* — 1 instance

To say an action is a good idea, English uses *it makes sense for us to…*; *that makes sense to us* only means "we understand it" and cannot take a *to*-verb after it. You said *makes sense* four times in this call, so the frame is worth getting right.

- "what you've shown us with your workflow around the IDP, **that** totally makes sense **to us** to follow the same process" → **given** what you've shown us with your workflow around AIDP, **it** totally makes sense **for us** to follow the same process

**Drill:** *It makes sense for us to wait.* · *It makes sense for them to start small.* · *That makes sense.* (full stop: I see, I agree). Either *that makes sense* with nothing after it, or *it makes sense for X to…*.
