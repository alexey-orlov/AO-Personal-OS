### Top items to study this session

#### 1. 🟡 Missing article before a noun — 6 instances

A singular countable noun needs *a* or *the*, and a case or project named after its client takes *the*. *In case of* is a different phrase: it means "if X happens"; for "regarding X" say *in the case of* or just *for*.

- "if you talk about **workforce optimization use case**" → "if you talk about **the** workforce optimization use case"
- "I'm thinking about **DHL case**" → "I'm thinking about **the** DHL case"
- "I also believe **in case of** many accounts there is just a generic name" → "I also believe **in the case of** many accounts there's just a generic name"

**Drill:** before each noun ask "one of many?" → *a*; "the one we both know?" → *the*. *The DHL case*, *the Bosch project*, *a standard problem*, *for the customer*, *in the case of*.

#### 2. 🟡 Yes/no question without inversion after *or* — 3 instances

A question keeps question word order in both halves of an *or*. With statement order in the second half (*or it is…*, *or we need…*), the question turns into a statement and the listener isn't sure you asked.

- "…and then position it as a technological novelty, **or it is not necessary**?" → "…**or is it not necessary**?"
- "Do we have something drafted separately for these two topics, **or it's just— or we don't have anything yet**?" → "…**or don't we have anything yet**?"
- "Is it available somewhere in our CRM, **or we need** to figure out…?" → "…**or do we need** to figure out…?"

**Drill:** after *or* in a question, flip the next two words: *or is it*, *or do we*, *or is there*, *or are they*. Never *or it is*, *or we need*.

#### 3. 🟡 Indirect question with question word order — 3 instances

The mirror rule of item 2: when the question sits inside a sentence (*I'm missing…*, *figure out…*, *trying to understand…*), it takes statement order and drops *do*.

- "I'm missing some criteria how they assess that and what are the, **what are the others submitting**" → "…and **what the others are submitting**"
- "or we need to figure out **where are their headquarters**" → "or do we need to figure out **where their headquarters are**"
- "I'm just trying to understand **how do I pick** from this list the accounts" → "I'm just trying to understand **how to pick** from this list the accounts"

**Drill:** *Where are they?* but *I don't know where they are.* *How do I pick?* but *I'm trying to understand how to pick.* After *know, understand, figure out, wonder, ask*: no inversion, no *do*.

#### 4. 🟡 Set phrase: *on the surface* — 2 instances

The idiom for "at first glance" is *on the surface*; *at the surface* is a physical place (at the surface of the water), and *layer* makes it more literal still.

- "**at the surface** there was no engineering novelty there" → "**on the surface** there was no engineering novelty"
- "So **at the surface layer**, it's a business challenge we solved" → "So **on the surface**, it's a business challenge we solved"

**Drill:** *on the surface*, *on the face of it*, *at first glance*: all mean "before you look closer".

#### 5. 🟡 Missing preposition — 2 instances

A noun followed by a question word needs a preposition to attach it (*criteria for how*), and a deadline phrase needs one too (*by your morning*).

- "I'm missing **some criteria how** they assess that" → "I'm missing **the criteria for how** they assess it"
- "you get back to us **your morning** and then we proceed" → "you get back to us **by your morning** and then we proceed"

**Drill:** *criteria for how*, *a question about whether*, *an answer to what*; *by your morning*, *by end of day*, *in your morning*.

#### 6. 🟡 *what … looks like*, not *how … looks like* — 1 instance

English says *what it looks like* or *how it looks*; *how it looks like* blends the two (a calque of *как выглядит*). One instance, but it is among the most recognisable non-native markers.

- "Do we have a reference **of how** the output artifact should look like?" → "Do we have a reference **for what** the output artifact should look like?"

**Drill:** *What does it look like?* · *How does it look?* · *Show me what the poster looks like.* *Like* goes with *what*, never with *how*.

#### 7. 🟡 *even though … but* — 1 instance

English uses one linking word per contrast: *even though X, Y* or *X, but Y*. Both together is a calque of *хотя…, но…*.

- "**even though** it was not completed, **but** it was designed for Bellron" → "**even though** it wasn't completed, it was designed for Belron"

**Drill:** pick one: *Even though it wasn't finished, it was designed for Belron.* or *It wasn't finished, but it was designed for Belron.* Never both.
