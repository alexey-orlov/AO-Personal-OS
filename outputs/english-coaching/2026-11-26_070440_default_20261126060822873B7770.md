## Executive summary

Grammar (§4) carries the most issues, and two patterns drive it. Direct questions often lack subject-verb inversion ("What you are still doing manually?", "or it's only…?"). Conditionals often carry *will*/*would* in the if-clause, or real-tense verbs where the meaning is hypothetical. Wrong words and collocations (§1) come second: verb-noun pairs a native speaker wouldn't choose (*do features*, *engineers we never face*, *I see another question*) and RU/UA calques (*how do you think*, *open nature*, *for my self-education*). The most important fix is the long discovery question. Three questions (turns on the testing obstacle, on-site access, and Maximo's engineers) ran past 100 words, stacked two or three questions inside one sentence and left the engineers to work out what was being asked. Ask one question per sentence and put the question first. Severity totals: 🔴 3 · 🟡 74 · 🟢 36.

## Top items to study this session

#### 1. 🟡 Non-native verb-noun collocation: 12 instances

Each word is correct on its own, but a native speaker wouldn't pair them. These pairings make fluent speech sound translated. They are the biggest single source of "almost right" moments in this call.

- "software engineers who also **do some features**" → software engineers who also **build features**
- "hardware engineers **we never face**" → hardware engineers **we never deal with**
- "**I see another question** here" → **I have another question** here

**Drill:** *build/ship a feature*, *deal with / work with people*, *have a question*, *put together a proposal*, *give an example*, *when you get a chance*, *how much room for improvement*, *camera footage*.

#### 2. 🔴 Run-on / overlong question: 8 instances (3 🔴, 5 🟡)

When the question is the last of four or five clauses, the listener has to hold all of them before learning what to answer. Several of these turns stacked two or three questions in one sentence. The engineers answered part of the question, or answered a different one.

- "Curious, like, the key obstacle here is the, like, physical proximity… or the problem is not physical proximity but rather… because I'm interested in that because depending on that, the scope of potential improvements… might be different. If that's doable, because like…" → **What's the root cause: physical distance, or a lack of logs to trace?** The answer changes what we can offer.
- "…my key concern is, because maybe I'm not that experienced in this hardware interaction topic, my key concern and uncertainty for me is whether…" → **Is the blocker that only a human can see the robot, or that digital data exists on the device and you can't reach it?**
- "…would their answers be similar because they work on similar kinds of features, you just split them evenly and similar problems they face because…" → **Would Maximo's own engineers give the same answers, or are their problems different?**

**Drill:** question first, then the reason in one short clause: "**Is it X or Y?** I'm asking because…" Hold each turn to one question mark.

#### 3. 🟡 Direct question with no inversion: 8 instances

In a direct question, the auxiliary comes before the subject. Without the inversion, a question sounds like a statement. This includes the second half of "X, or Y?" questions, where you dropped the inversion every time.

- "**What you are** still doing manually and why?" → **What are you** still doing manually, and why?
- "**Why you're not** there yet?" → **Why aren't you** there yet?
- "do you think…, or **you see** the areas that AI will still be blind to" → …, or **do you see** areas that AI will still be blind to?

**Drill:** "…**or is it** …?", "…**or do you** …?", "…**or am I** wrong?" Rehearse these three endings until they come out automatically.

#### 4. 🟡 Conditionals: *will/would* after *if*, and real vs. hypothetical: 6 instances

The if-clause takes the present tense (real) or the past tense (hypothetical), never *will* or *would*. A hypothetical result takes *would*, not *will*.

- "I would really love **if Alexey will** also share" → I'd really love it **if Alexey would** also share / **if Alexey shared**
- "**if I'd ask** the engineers on Maximo's side" → **if I asked** the engineers on Maximo's side
- "**if you are** there next to the robot… would it also help?" → **if you were** there next to the robot… would it also help?

**Drill:** "If I **asked**… they **would**…", "If something **is** unclear, I'**ll** DM you." Nothing in the if-clause ends in -ll or -'d.

#### 5. 🟡 Wrong preposition in collocation: 6 instances

A verb or noun fixes its preposition, and RU/UA often chooses a different one.

- "briefly **elaborate about** the breakdown" → briefly **elaborate on** the breakdown
- "the root cause **for** those limitations" → the root cause **of** those limitations
- "how I was looking **onto** it" → how I was looking **at** it

**Drill:** *elaborate **on***, *root cause **of***, *look **at***, *specs **for***, *speak **from** your side*.

#### 6. 🟡 Linking an if-clause: *"if yes"* and *"…, and"* in place of *if so* and *then*: 6 instances

English joins an if-clause to its result with *then* or nothing, never *and*. *If yes* is a calque of «если да». The native form is *if so*.

- "did you ever discuss this, and **if yes, and** what was the challenge" → did you ever discuss this, and **if so, what** was the challenge?
- "But if there is nothing for an agent to look at…, **and** we are limited" → …, **then** we're limited
- "**If yes, then** did they have the same experience?" → **If so,** did they have the same experience?

**Drill:** "If so, …" / "If not, …" / "If that's the case, …", followed directly by the result.

#### 7. 🟡 RU/UA calque: 5 instances

These are literal translations with no English equivalent.

- "**how do you think**, how big is the room for improvement" → **what do you think**: how much room is there for improvement?
- "it's in **the open nature** in the desert" → it's **out in the open** in the desert
- "**for my self-education**" → **just for my own understanding**

**Drill:** "How do you think?" asks *by what method* you think. For an opinion, ask "**What** do you think?" Also: *outdoors / out in the open*, *where's the line*, *on top of that*.

#### 8. 🟡 Present perfect for an ongoing or unfinished period: 3 instances

Something that started in the past and continues now, or has no specific finished time, takes the present perfect.

- "you **are working** with them for a couple of years already" → you**'ve been working** with them for a couple of years
- "But you definitely **played** with Cursor" → But you**'ve** definitely **played** with Cursor
- "Nobody just **invested** in that" → Nobody**'s** really **invested** in that

**Drill:** *for / since / already / yet / ever* → reach for *have + -ed* (or *have been + -ing*).
